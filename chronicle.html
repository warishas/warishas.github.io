Case Study / 04 — Zero to One

Chronicle

Surgeons narrate hours of endoscopic footage into a handful of sentences in their operative notes, because nobody has time to re-watch a three-hour surgery. Chronicle uses AI to cut that footage into a reviewable highlight reel — giving surgeons a second, visual record alongside the note they already write.

The Question

How might digital imagery improve surgeons' recall, collaboration, and time — without becoming one more thing they have to manage?

Our sponsor had built a patent-pending algorithm that could compress an hours-long endoscopic surgical video down to fifteen minutes or less, isolating the phases and key moments a surgeon would actually want to see again. What didn't exist yet was any interface for a surgeon to use it. We were handed a capable model and an open question: what does a surgeon actually do with a video, and where does it belong in a workflow built entirely around written notes?

Grounding the Problem Before Designing Anything

We spent the first third of the project refusing to sketch a single screen until we understood the world we were designing for. That discipline came from the whole team, and I was one of the designers doing the legwork alongside our researcher.

Contextual inquiry at Harborview

We spent time on-site in the operating room and surrounding spaces, mapping the end-to-end journey of a surgeon through handoffs and consultations, and watching where video references already showed up — or conspicuously didn't.

In-depth interviews

One-on-one conversations with surgeons who perform endoscopic surgery and consultations went deeper: how they take notes mid-procedure, how they use (or avoid) digital imagery, and where AI could plausibly fit into that without adding friction to an already time-pressured job.

Our research question evolved as we learned — it started broad ("what's a typical day like for a surgeon?") and narrowed as patterns emerged, eventually converging on the question that shaped the whole rest of the project: what is the value of a video for a surgeon, specifically in their workflow?

Surgeons will be spending additional time adding video to their notes, so reducing friction is paramount.

Synthesized Research Insight

Designing With the Team

Our process was a deliberate cycle of divergence and convergence: independent work during the week, then a working session to debrief sponsor and instructor feedback and reconcile what each of us had explored. I contributed sketches and interaction concepts at the wireframe and mid-fidelity stages, where two or three of us would rapidly iterate on the same flow in parallel to stress-test alternatives before converging on one direction.

Two insights from concept testing with our sponsor, a practicing surgeon, directly shaped the interface:

  • Side-by-side comparison between the written note and the video kept surgeons oriented — video as a supplement to the note, never a replacement for it.
  • Surgeons already follow an internal checklist while operating. The AI's first-pass edit needed to echo that existing structure, not impose a new one.

Keeping the Surgeon in Control

The most senior-level tension in this project wasn't visual — it was about trust in an automated system making editorial decisions about a legal medical record. Every design conversation about the AI's output came back to the same constraint: the surgeon needed to stay the final authority over what the video showed and how it was used, not the algorithm.

We used an AI Trust Score during evaluative testing specifically to measure that — not just "does this look good," but "do you believe what this is showing you, and would you rely on it." That surfaced a concrete usability gap: the right rail's edit-versus-add functions weren't clearly differentiated, and clips in the list lacked enough affordance to tell which one was currently in view. Both went back into the next iteration.

Final Design

A web application that lets surgeons view and manage surgical video highlight reels as a supplement to their operative notes.

Using the sponsor's AI compression model, Chronicle reduces hours of raw endoscopic footage into a fifteen-minute (or shorter) reel, organized around the recognizable phases and key moments of the surgery. Surgeons can review, reference, and eventually cite these clips the same way they'd reference a written note — closing the gap between what happened in the OR and what makes it into the record.

Where It Landed

  • Usability evaluation showed a positive directional signal for the MVP experience — surgeons could navigate the reel and understood the video's relationship to their notes.
  • The clearest near-term use case that emerged was educational and academic reference, with surgeons expressing real appetite to eventually extend it into operative notes themselves.
  • We handed the requirements document and high-fidelity Figma prototype to our sponsor for their development team to carry into implementation.

This was a capstone project delivered to a sponsor, not a shipped commercial product — outcomes reflect usability evaluation and sponsor validation, not production usage data.

Reflection

Chronicle taught me how to design for trust in a system that makes decisions a human used to make alone. The interesting design problem was never "how do we show a video" — it was how to make an AI's editorial judgment legible and overridable enough that a surgeon, whose license is on the line, would actually rely on it. That's a very different kind of craft than visual polish, and it's the one I most want to keep practicing.
